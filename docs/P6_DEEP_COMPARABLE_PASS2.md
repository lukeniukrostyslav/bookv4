# P6 DEEP COMPARABLE PASS 2 — US MARKET

Date: 2026-09-21

## Important research rule

This pass records real comparable titles and patterns found in current U.S. publishing/bestseller sources. It does not claim direct Kindle unit sales unless a source explicitly provides an estimate, and third-party estimates are labeled as such.

## Concept A — The Woman in the Family Photo

### Closest observed patterns

- *Just One Look* by Harlan Coben: an old photo triggers a disappearance and exposes buried past events. Publishers Weekly described the premise as simple but noted limited originality and a complicated plot. This is a strong warning that "mysterious old family photo" by itself is not sufficiently differentiated.
- *The Last Time We Saw Her* by Jaclyn Goldis (2026): a woman revisits the disappearance of her sister, with memory loss, unreliable narrators and a reopened case. This overlaps with family disappearance + memory/reality uncertainty.
- *Rupture* by Ragnar Jónasson: an old family photo is part of a cold-case investigation. This demonstrates that the photo-as-clue device already exists in mystery.
- *Mother Daughter Sister Stranger* by Hank Phillippi Ryan (2026): a woman searches for her missing sister while old family secrets emerge.
- *The Lies Between Us* by Jen Bray (2026): a retired police officer searches for her missing sister while a strange ability exposes lies.

### Implication

The original concept must NOT rely on "a strange photo reveals a missing family member" alone. That territory is already well represented.

Potential differentiation would require a second mechanism that changes the mystery fundamentally.

## Concept B — The Last Voicemail

### Closest observed patterns

- *Missing Sister* by Joshilyn Jackson (2026): the protagonist's dead twin leaves a cryptic voicemail, and the story develops into a revenge/murder investigation.
- *The Bone Thief* by Vanessa Lillie: a voicemail launches an investigation into remains and a missing woman.
- *The Red House* by Mary Morris: a voicemail from the detective connected to a decades-old disappearance triggers a family investigation.
- *Bloodline* by Felix Francis: unanswered/ignored phone messages and a sister's apparent death become part of an investigation.
- *I Didn't Do It* uses voicemail transcripts among documentary evidence, showing the device is familiar in mystery construction.

### Implication

"Voicemail from a missing/dead person" is commercially understandable but is NOT sufficiently novel on its own. The concept needs an unusual rule, e.g. the message arrives under a verifiable impossible condition or contains information that changes the identity of the person believed to be missing.

## Concept C — Tomorrow's Missing Person

### Closest observed patterns

- *The Gone World* by Tom Sweterlitsch: a missing-person investigation is combined with future/time-travel mechanics. The premise demonstrates the strength and complexity of speculative crime.
- *The Tomorrow Project* by H Critchlow (2026): a near-future thriller structured around crisis, disappearance and future consequences.
- *Augusta Pine Does Not Exist* by Emily Lloyd-Jones (2026): identity itself can be erased/reassigned through technology in a near-future thriller.
- Current 2026 thriller previews also include speculative/high-concept premises such as *The Chain House* and other technology/reality-driven suspense concepts.

### Implication

This candidate has the clearest high-concept separation from ordinary domestic thriller patterns, but it carries a major burden: the "database of tomorrow's missing people" needs strict rules. If the mechanism can predict anything without cost or limitation, suspense collapses.

A strong version would make the prediction mechanism limited, fallible, or costly.

## Concept D — The Neighbor Who Doesn't Exist

### Closest observed patterns

- *The Neighbors Are Watching* by Aggie Blum Thompson (2026): suburban neighbors, murder, memory gaps and secrets. Publishers Weekly considered the subject matter familiar/stale in this execution.
- *A Neighbor's Guide to Murder* by Louise Candlish (2026): apartment neighbors, murder, documents/manuscript framing and questions about reliability.
- *Too Close to Home* by Seraphina Nova Glass (2026): suburban women, a missing neighbor, secrets and escalating danger.
- *The Boy Next Door* is an older example of a neighbor-centered premise, demonstrating the longevity of the neighbor hook.
- *She Didn't See It Coming* by Shari Lapena uses a suburban murder with neighbors, family and friends as suspects.

### Implication

"Something is wrong with the neighbor" is a crowded domestic-thriller zone. The exact contradiction in our concept is interesting, but it must lead to a mechanism that is more original than unreliable narration or a hidden identity.

## Cross-concept finding

The most important discovery is that **the strongest-sounding hooks are often already represented in the market**.

Therefore, P6 should not choose a concept merely because its one-sentence hook sounds exciting.

The next concept-design iteration should use a two-layer hook:

**familiar commercial promise + unusual story mechanism**

Examples of mechanism categories to investigate:
- time-limited information;
- evidence that changes after being observed;
- multiple people receiving different versions of the same event;
- a digital record that contradicts physical reality;
- a mystery where solving the first mystery creates the second;
- a rule that the protagonist must obey to keep the evidence.

## Current status

No candidate is approved.

The four concepts remain useful as market-learning prototypes, but all four require additional differentiation before approval.
